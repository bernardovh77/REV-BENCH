# REV-BENCH reviewed: concept, architecture and structure (2026-11-15)

A review of the whole project by Claude (Fable 5.2), asked for by Bernardo before stage 066.
It reads the repo as it is at commit `ac826fd`: 648 tracked files, 103 commits since
2026-10-16, a core of about 10,000 lines of C, 11,400 lines of C tests, 14,000 lines of Python
tools and about 140 documents.

Each problem has the same four parts: what it is, the evidence in this repo, how real projects
handle it, and a solution with the steps to build it. The problems are ordered by how much they
threaten the project's goal, not by how easy they are. Nothing here changes PLAN.md: a solution
Bernardo adopts gets a stage number there (127 onward).

Where I state something I did not measure, I say so.

**What became of each problem** is at the end, under "Since this review": stage 127 fixed
what could be fixed before stage 066 and gave the rest a stage.

## The verdict in short

**What is strong, and should not change:**

- **One physical engine owns everything.** The crank, the gas and the sound come from the same
  state, so a change to a cam or a header changes the torque and the sound together. Most
  engine-sound programs can't do that.
- **The layering.** Core (rb.h) → real-time host → add-on → engine process → sandboxed window.
  Each layer is tested alone, and no physics or audio runs in JavaScript. This is how
  professional audio software is built.
- **The engineering habits.** No allocation in the core, exact repeatable renders, planted bugs
  that prove the tests can fail, a speed check that fails at 3 %, CI on two systems.
- **Honesty about results.** Stage 118 states its misses instead of hiding them. Rejected
  experiments are kept and named as rejected.

**What threatens it, in one paragraph.** The project's stated first priority is sound that
Bernardo's ears accept, and that is the part furthest from done and on the longest path. The
engine has no speed to spare on the baseline laptop while every later phase adds cost. There
is no recording of a known engine to measure against. And the process around the code has
grown heavier than the code. The eleven problems below are those four, taken apart.

## 1. The sound is reached by the longest path

**What.** Bernardo said the sound is the top priority. His complaints are specific: dull at
idle, repeats too exactly, the four is too quiet, the V8's crackle. The plan answers them with
phase B: fourteen gated stages (050–063) that characterise mufflers, radiation, flow noise and
structure, integrate research solvers, compare renderers, promote one, then ask him to listen.
His ears come at the end of the chain (063), not inside each step.

**Evidence here.**
- The research solvers in `experiments/acoustics/` (2,700 lines, stages 043–049 and 114) run
  "far below real time and only on weak, cool waves" (PLAN.md). Two candidates were already
  rejected (031, and 043's A2). The chain's outcome may be "no winner", which stage 060 allows.
- The changes that did move the sound were small and direct: stage 12b's noise layers
  (−59 → −41 dB above 2 kHz), stage 12c's variation, stage 115's jet-noise fix. None needed a
  new solver.
- RESEARCH.md already records how the best-known program does it.

**Real examples.**
- **engine-sim** (AngeTheGreat) is the program most people call realistic. Its physics stops at
  1.9 kHz. Everything above comes from time jitter, noise multiplied into the flow, and a
  convolution with an impulse response (RESEARCH.md, "How others get there"). It shipped that
  and improved it in public.
- **ensim4** uses 1D pipe gas dynamics like REV-BENCH, and credits engine-sim for the impulse
  convolution it adds at the end.
- **Most racing games** use recordings cut into loops or grains, placed by rpm and load. No
  physics, but they sound real because they are recordings.
- **Engineering tools** (GT-POWER, Ricardo WAVE) solve 1D gas dynamics offline for
  engineering accuracy. They predict the low firing orders at the tailpipe; they do not try to
  make listenable audio.

So nobody gets listenable realism from 1D gas dynamics alone. The ones that sound real add a
linear filter and randomness on top. REV-BENCH's "stay physical, add layers" rule is the right
one, but phase B reads as if a better solver will deliver the sound.

**Solution: put the ears inside the loop, and take the short fixes first.**

1. Finish 066 with an A/B switch (below). Live play is the tool phase B lacked.
2. Before 050, run a short stage, **"the complaints list"**: for each complaint, the shortest
   physically honest fix, listened to live. A candidate list, to be measured, not assumed:
   - *Dull idle from behind:* a body and room response. A linear filter is physics too: the
     tailpipe's radiation, the car's underside and the ground are a transfer function. Compute
     it from geometry where possible (the listener already has the ground image); otherwise
     fit a short filter to recordings and say so.
   - *Repeats too exactly:* stage 12c's variation acts on the burn. Turbulence also modulates
     the pulses on their way down the pipe (RESEARCH.md's Acta Acustica finding: random,
     weakly correlated modulation per partial). Add slow random modulation of the pipes'
     losses or wall temperature, seeded.
   - *The four is quiet:* measure its level against the recordings at the same distance before
     touching anything. 066 fixes only the headroom.
   - *The V8's crackle:* render the overrun with each layer alone (`--layers`) to find which
     one makes it.
3. Keep phase B's gates for anything that replaces the pipes' solver. Do not require them for
   a layer that can be switched off.

**How to build the A/B switch.** Add one parameter, `RB_PARAM_VARIANT` (an integer, 0 = today's
sound), in `rb_params.c`, applied in `rb_sim_apply` without a rebuild. Each candidate reads it.
The app gets a key that flips it while the engine runs. The switch is the listening test: no
WAV files to carry, and Bernardo can do it blind (the app picks at random which is A).
Candidates that lose are deleted and their variant number retired, as experiments are now.

## 2. No matched reference recording

**What.** The sound is measured against Freesound recordings of unknown engines, exhausts,
microphones and distances. Stage 039 has been open for this since October.

**Evidence here.** The target ranges are as wide as the unknowns: energy above 2 kHz "−42 to
−6 dB", cycle repeat "−2 to +13 dB". A render can sit inside a 36 dB range and still sound
wrong. The numbers can reject a bad sound but cannot confirm a good one. CODEX.md notes that
Bernardo's own recordings are of turbocharged cars while the presets are naturally aspirated.

**Real examples.** Engineering tools are validated against a test cell: the same engine, a
microphone at a stated distance, the speed logged. Hobby simulators are tuned by ear against
videos. REV-BENCH's claim ("physical") needs the first kind, at least once.

**Solution: one matched recording is worth more than fifty unmatched.**

1. Pick the preset closest to a car Bernardo can reach (his, a friend's, a relative's): a
   naturally aspirated four is the likely one.
2. Record it standing still, engine warm, bonnet shut: a phone or recorder on a stand 3 m
   behind the tailpipe, 1.2 m high (the default `RB_HEAR_BEHIND`). Three takes: 30 s idle, 10 s
   held at 3000 rpm, three blips to 4000. Clap once at the start for sync.
3. Log the speed. A Bluetooth OBD reader and a logging app give rpm against time. Without
   one, the firing order's frequency in the recording gives rpm (`tools/orders.py` already
   tracks orders).
4. Write down the car, engine code, exhaust (stock or not), the surface and the phone model.
   Phones compress and filter: record in the least processed mode it has, and treat the level
   as relative, not absolute.
5. Set the preset's geometry to that engine's (bore, stroke, cams if known, pipe lengths
   measured with a tape) and replay the logged rpm through `rb_cli sound`.
6. Compare with `tools/compare_real.py`, now one-to-one. This closes 039 for one engine and
   gives 059 something to stand on.

A second recording at 0.5 m from the tailpipe separates the exhaust from the car's body.

## 3. No speed headroom, and every later phase costs more

**What.** On the baseline laptop's fast cores the engine runs at 1.28–2.23 times real time at
the reduced quality, and the V8s miss the 1.5 target. At high quality only the four and the
boxer are above 1.0. All of it is on one thread.

**Evidence here.**
- Stage 118's profile: no hot spot above 12 %. The cost is spread over the thermodynamics of
  about 400 pipe cells: a mixture of three species and a Newton solve for temperature, in
  every cell, every step. The bit-identical speed-ups are used up (+3–11 %).
- The plan adds cost: turbo (more volumes and a shaft), V10 and V12 (more cylinders and
  runners: by proportion a V12 would be under real time; not measured), a promoted renderer
  (062), and a 3D view sharing the same two fast cores.
- The old target (4 times natively) is recorded as unmet.

**Real examples.**
- **engine-sim** runs about 80,000 steps a second with a far cruder gas model (RESEARCH.md).
- **Digital waveguides** (the Baldan paper in RESEARCH.md, and physical-modelling synthesis
  in general) carry a wave down a pipe with one delay line each way. The cost per pipe is two
  reads and two writes a sample, whatever its length, and a delay line carries every
  frequency up to half the sample rate.
- **Exhaust engineers** commonly split the problem the same way: nonlinear gas dynamics where
  the waves are strong, linear acoustics (transfer matrices) for mufflers and tailpipes.

**Solution: make the cold end of the exhaust linear.** After the catalyst the waves are weak
and the gas is cool: exactly the range the research solvers are valid in. Those cells are a
large share of the 400. Replacing them with delay lines removes their cost and, as a side
effect, the 2.4 kHz limit of the 7.5 cm grid for that part of the path.

The repo already has the pieces: `wave.c` (the delay-line graph), `coupled_duct.c` (the proven
hand-off from nonlinear cells to characteristics), `junction.c`. What is missing is the
decision to use them for speed first, as a quality tier, instead of waiting for stage 060 to
judge them as a replacement for everything.

**How to build it** (a stage of its own, before 062):

1. **Measure the premise.** In today's engine, log the pressure swing and Mach number in each
   exhaust cell for all five presets at full load and on the overrun. Draw where the swing
   falls under 5 % of the mean. That line is the boundary; if it is not near the catalyst,
   the plan changes.
2. **One pipe first.** Replace only the four's tailpipe with a delay-line pair, joined to the
   last nonlinear cell by characteristics (the incoming and outgoing wave from p and u). Keep
   the mean flow as a slow term so the back pressure stays right.
3. **Prove the books.** The engine's torque, VE and back pressure must stay within 1 %; the
   mass leaving the tailpipe must match the mass entering the linear part over a cycle.
4. **Compare the sound** with `tools/tier_compare.py` against high quality, then live with
   the A/B switch.
5. Only then the muffler (a chambered can is a junction of delay lines with a volume) and the
   exhaust pipes.
6. Declare it as a tier with its misses stated, like stage 118.

**Cheaper things to try first**, each a day or two:
- Problem 4 below (it may make slower pipes usable at once).
- **Cells that only hold burned gas.** A cell far down the exhaust rarely sees fuel or fresh
  air, so its mixture is constant and `rb_gas_init` (8–11 % of the time) is wasted there.
  Skip the remix while a cell's composition has not changed. This must stay bit-identical;
  `tools/cli_parity.py` proves it. (Stage 118 tried a lazy *half* of `rb_gas_init` and it was
  slower; this is a different cut, but measure before believing it.)
- **A second thread per exhaust bank** is not worth it: the banks meet at X and H pipes, and
  exact repeatability across threads is hard. I would not do it.

## 4. The listener's hand-off from the pipes may be making false treble

**What.** This is a hypothesis from stage 118's V8 search, not a finding. With the pipes at
16 kHz the engine's torque stayed within 1 % but the sound above 4 kHz rose 13–44 dB. That
pattern (physics right, treble wrong, worse the slower the pipes) is what a hold does: if the
listener repeats each pipe-rate flow value until the next arrives, the staircase puts a copy
of the spectrum around each multiple of the pipe rate. At 48 kHz the copies are above hearing
and the output filter removes them. At 24 kHz they land at 21–24 kHz. At 16 kHz they land at
13–16 kHz, where they are heard.

**Why it matters.** If true, it is a cheap fix that makes slower pipes usable, which is the
V8s' missing speed.

**Real examples.** Every sample-rate converter interpolates between samples for this reason.
The noise path in `audio.c` (`noise_arriving`) already interpolates linearly between two ring
entries; the flow path should be checked for the same.

**How to test and fix it:**

1. **Test first.** A unit test that pushes a pure 1 kHz sine into the listener's flow ring at
   a pipe rate of 16 kHz and renders. With a hold, lines appear at 15 and 17 kHz, about 24 dB
   under the tone; with interpolation they are far lower. If the lines are not there, the
   hypothesis is wrong: record that and close it.
2. **Fix.** Interpolate the flow between the last two pipe-rate values across the block's
   physics steps. Linear interpolation costs one multiply and delays the flow by one pipe
   step, which the listener's delays can absorb.
3. **This changes every quality's sound slightly**, so it is a sound change: renders, the
   comparison tools and Bernardo's ears, per CLAUDE.md. Then rerun stage 118's V8 table with
   the pipes at 16 and 19.2 kHz.

## 5. The gas scheme runs at its stability edge, held by guards

**What.** The core integrates with explicit Euler on a staggered grid and stays stable by
running at 192 kHz and by guards: the flow cap, the equalisation limit, the sonic guard
(`RB_PIPE_MOST_SONIC`), the area-ratio limit (`RB_PIPE_ONWARD_MAX_RATIO`).

**Evidence here.** CLAUDE.md's own history: at 96 kHz the valves' cells are "too stiff for the
flow cap"; a throttle-shut runner ran away, "gas at 13 K", before the sonic guard; 75 mm
tailpipes on the V8 blew up across a 1.86 area step. Each was found by the fuzzer or by
accident, then guarded.

**Why it matters now.** Stage 067 gives the user an editor. Users will type the engines the
fuzzer has not met. A guard that fires silently makes a sound that is wrong without anyone
knowing; a missing guard makes a NaN, which the core turns into silence.

**Real examples.** Commercial 1D codes use the same family of scheme (explicit, staggered,
limited by the Courant condition) and handle it by refusing or subdividing: the time step
follows the smallest cell, and the solver reports which part limited it. Hobby simulators
clamp and carry on, and their users post engines that explode.

**Solution: keep the scheme, make the guards visible, and bound the editor by what was
tested.**

1. **Count every guard.** Stage 038's diagnostics have slots; make sure each guard increments
   one, and add a telemetry value, guard interventions per second.
2. **Show it.** The app's readout (068) shows a warning when it is above a threshold: "this
   build is outside what the model handles well". That is honest, and it is what
   combustionlab.net was criticised for lacking (RESEARCH.md).
3. **Fuzz the editor's ranges.** Make `rb_cli fuzz` draw each field from its range in
   `FIELDS[]` (the ranges the editor will offer) and run it nightly in CI for an hour. A range
   that produces NaNs or a high guard rate is narrowed in `FIELDS[]`.
4. **Refuse at the door.** `rb_engine_desc_check` already rejects some descriptions; add the
   ones the fuzzer finds (a cell too short for the time step, an area step over the limit)
   with a message the editor can show.

## 6. One release carries everything

**What.** The first public release is stage 102, after the editor, the sound chain, 3D, turbo,
knock and damage, more engines, saving and sharing, the port and signing. About sixty stages
remain before anyone outside this machine runs it.

**Evidence here.** Bernardo's first session with stage 124's build produced three findings no
test had (the four's level, the throttle, the crackle). One user, one session.

**Real examples.** engine-sim went public early, with few engines and no editor (engines are
script files), and its users' reports shaped what came after. BeamNG.drive and Automation
both sold early-access builds for years.

**Solution: make 069 a real public pre-release.** The plan already calls it "the first
pre-release"; make that mean a GitHub release others can download.

1. Decide the licence first (problem 11).
2. 069's installer goes up as `v0.1.0`, marked pre-release, with a plain README: what works,
   what doesn't, which machines play which engines.
3. A "report a problem" link that opens a GitHub issue template asking for the engine, the
   settings and the CPU.
4. Each later gate (063, 077, 084, 094) becomes a version. Until stage 101 signs the
   installer, the README explains Windows' unsigned-app warning.

Publishing is Bernardo's call each time (CLAUDE.md).

## 7. Two fast cores for audio, the window and 3D

**What.** The baseline laptop (i7-1355U) has two performance cores. The render thread is now
kept on them. Chromium's renderer and GPU process will want them too, and phase C adds a 3D
view at display rate.

**Evidence here.** Stage 118: a V8 block takes up to 15 ms at high quality; the efficiency
cores run at about 0.55 times. Stage 077, which qualifies audio and 3D together, is the last
stage of phase C.

**Real examples.** Digital audio workstations face this daily: the audio thread gets the
priority, the interface gets what is left and drops frames first. For REV-BENCH the sound is
the product, so it should behave like one of those.

**Solution: test the conflict now, with a fake load.**

1. In 069's stress run, add a mode where the window draws a deliberately heavy animation (a
   canvas redrawn every frame with a few thousand shapes). Fifteen minutes, count underruns.
2. If it underruns: slow the window, don't push the audio thread harder. Cap the view's frame
   rate when the host reports its ring below half.
3. Put the host's ring level in what the window receives (it is one number), so the 3D view
   can shed load before the sound breaks.
4. Carry the result into 070's design instead of discovering it in 077.

## 8. Throttle input lives in the window

**What.** Stage 066 reads the keyboard, mouse and game controller in the window (the Gamepad
API) and sends the throttle to the engine process. It also requires the sound to go on when
the window is minimised or hidden.

**The flaw.** Chromium slows timers and stops animation frames in hidden windows, and a page
that loses focus stops getting key-up events. So with the window hidden or behind another,
the throttle can freeze at its last value: an engine held at full throttle with no way to
lift, until the limiter. I have not tested this in the app, and `app/src` sets no
`backgroundThrottling` option today.

**Real examples.** Games read controllers in the game loop through the system's input API,
not through a web page. Driving simulators add a dead-man rule: lost input means throttle
zero.

**Solution, in 066:**

1. **A dead-man rule in the engine process, not the window.** While a "live input" style is
   active (ramped key, analog key, trigger), the window sends the throttle at a steady beat.
   If none arrives for 250 ms, the engine process ramps the throttle to zero over 100 ms. The
   latched and on/off styles are exempt: they hold by design.
2. **On blur or hide,** the window sends "released" for every held key and trigger.
3. Test both end to end: hide the window with the key held; the snapshot's throttle must
   reach zero.
4. **The Razer keyboard.** Through Synapse its analog travel reaches a page as an emulated
   controller, so the same focus rule applies. Whether Razer offers a native analog SDK is
   checked in 066, as the plan says. If native input is ever added, it belongs in the engine
   process behind `host/src/platform_*.c`, and the dead-man rule already covers it.

## 9. macOS and Linux arrive at the end

**What.** The app is built Windows first, and stage 126 ports it after phases A–F. Linux CI
builds the C, the add-on and the interface's tests. macOS runs only the core, weekly, and that
run has never fired.

**Evidence here.** Stage 122 met MSVC differences at once (the clock, library calls,
last-bit rounding). Stage 124's install check found a Windows short-path bug only in CI. Each
system brings its own surprises; meeting them sixty stages late means meeting them together.

**Real examples.** Electron apps that add macOS late meet a known list: signing and
notarisation, universal binaries, the real-time thread API, menu conventions, native add-ons
under the hardened runtime.

**Solution: a thin port now, the full port at 126.**

1. Run the macOS workflow by hand today (Actions → ci → Run workflow) and fix what it finds.
   Check why the schedule did not fire.
2. Add a macOS job on every push that builds the core, `host/` on the null device and the
   add-on, and runs their tests. No packaging, no signing.
3. Add "the app starts and plays on the null device" as an end-to-end smoke test on Linux,
   and on macOS once step 2 is green.
4. Stage 126 keeps what needs real machines: thread priorities, devices, packages, signing.

## 10. The process outweighs the code

**What.** The rules, logs and records around the code are now bigger than the code, and they
cost time and attention every stage.

**Evidence here.**
- `CLAUDE.md` is 494 lines, loaded into every session. Most of it is the history of stages
  (what stage 10 did to the idle valve), not rules. The rules are scattered inside it.
- `CODEX.md` is 1,849 lines; there are about 100 stage records; the roadmap is 14 catalogs
  plus a legacy map across three numbering schemes (1–13 with 12b and 13b-r, S1a–S4, then
  001–126).
- The planted-bug tables match source text, so they go stale whenever code moves: 29 in stage
  120, more in 123 and 124. Each stage pays to retarget them.
- Rules that live only in prose get broken. In this session I pushed a commit with the host
  suite failing, because nothing but a sentence stopped me.
- Two agents with different styles: stage 119 existed partly to reformat one agent's C to the
  other's. There is no `.clang-format`.

**Real examples.** Large C projects keep the rules a machine can check in the machine (a
formatter, a hook, CI) and keep prose for what only a person can judge. SQLite, which tests as
heavily as this project, runs its mutation tests from a script instead of keeping a
hand-written list.

**Solution: move rules from prose into checks, and history out of the instructions.**

1. **Split CLAUDE.md.** Keep under 150 lines: the workflow, the hard rules (no allocation, no
   physics in JavaScript, never play on a real device, commit style) and pointers. Move the
   module reference into `docs/architecture.md` (one section per module, updated by the stage
   that changes it). The stage history is already in `docs/stage-NN.md`.
2. **A pre-push hook** (`.githooks/pre-push`, enabled with `git config core.hooksPath
   .githooks`): runs the quick suites (`python tools/run_tests.py native python host`) and
   refuses the push on failure. The full run stays the stage's last step.
3. **`.clang-format`** matching stage 119's style, checked in CI with
   `clang-format --dry-run -Werror`. Introduce it in a commit that changes nothing but
   whitespace, proven byte-identical as stage 119 was.
4. **Make planted bugs survive a reformat.** (Corrected in stage 127: a bug's text already
   matched anywhere in its file, so a move inside a file never broke one, and anchoring to
   functions would have gained nothing. What breaks them is changed spacing and changed
   words.) Match a bug's text with any run of spaces and line breaks standing for any other.
   Later, generate simple mutants (flip a sign, swap `<` and `<=`, drop a term) in named
   physics functions and report which survive: that finds weak tests nobody thought to plant.
5. **Archive CODEX.md** entries older than a month into `docs/log/`.

## 11. Housekeeping

Each is small. Together they take an afternoon.

**No licence in a public repo.** There is no `LICENSE` file and the README names none. A
public repo without a licence is "all rights reserved": nobody may legally reuse it or
contribute to it, and visitors can't tell what they may do. engine-sim and ensim4 are MIT.
This is Bernardo's decision, with three honest options: MIT (open, like its peers), a
source-available licence (readable, not reusable), or "all rights reserved" stated in the
README. Then add the file, the `license` field in `app/package.json` and a line in the README.

**Stray folders at the repo's root.** `C:UsersbernaAppDataLocalTemptmp3ujjirhlbuild/` and one
like it (2026-11-01) hold only empty folders. Some tool passed a Windows temp path through a
POSIX shell, which ate the backslashes, and make created the result relative to the repo.
Delete both; then find the tool (likely a Python test that builds in a temporary folder and
calls make) and pass the path with forward slashes, as CLAUDE.md already requires. A test in
the Python suite can assert that the repo's root has no entry starting with `C:`.

**The old browser scaffold.** `web/` (3,600 lines) plays a test tone, and every stage keeps
its Playwright test green in two modes. Keep the WebAssembly build and its unit tests: they
are a real portability check. Retire the tone page's end-to-end test until stage 125 needs a
browser page again.

**`engine.c` is 1,674 lines and owns the whole step.** Turbo, knock and limits will each add
to it. Before phase D, split it along the step order CLAUDE.md already describes: the
computer (idle, spark, cuts), the intake side, the exhaust side, leaving `engine.c` the cycle
and its books. A pure move, proven as stage 119 was: byte-identical objects and renders.
